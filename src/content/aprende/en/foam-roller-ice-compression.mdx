There's a whole industry around recovery: foam rollers, massage guns, ice baths, compression socks, pressure boots. The useful question isn't whether they work, but what exactly they improve.

## What the meta-analyses say

They agree with each other fairly well. Foam rolling reduces perceived muscle soreness by a small but consistent amount, and its effect on performance is close to nothing. Cold water reduces perceived soreness and swelling. Compression reduces perceived fatigue and some swelling.

<Callout>They all improve how you feel. None of them shows the muscle is repaired sooner.</Callout>

Being “only” a feeling doesn't make it useless: feeling better the next day is the difference between going out and not going out, and going out is what trains you. But it's worth buying with your eyes open.

## A caveat about cold

Cold water after strength training may interfere with part of the adaptation you were after: inflammation isn't only damage, it's also the signal that triggers improvement. If the day included a strength session and you want that session's benefits, an ice bath isn't the best idea.

## What actually recovers you and isn't for sale

- Sleep. It's the only one with a large effect, and it's free.
- Eating enough. You can't recover from a load you don't feed.
- Easy days that are genuinely easy. Most people who don't recover aren't missing a foam roller: their easy days aren't easy.

## What to do tomorrow

- If the roller relieves you and you enjoy it, use it. Don't expect it to make you faster.
- Before spending on recovery, look at how much you sleep and how much you eat.
- Save the ice bath for race days or very high load, not as routine after strength work.
