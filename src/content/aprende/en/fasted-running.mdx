The idea is logical, which is why it spread: if you go out without breakfast, your body has no easy sugar to hand and pulls from fat. And that's true. The problem is the next sentence, the one nobody checks: that this makes you lose more.

## The part that's true

A meta-analysis of twenty-seven studies confirms it: exercising easy or moderate while fasted oxidises more fat for as long as the exercise lasts. The difference is real and measurable.

## The part that isn't

When you look at the result over weeks instead of that one hour, the difference vanishes. Reviews comparing fasted and fed training find no greater loss of weight or body fat in the fasted group.

<Callout>Burning fat for an hour and losing fat over a month are not the same thing. The body does the sums at the end of the day, not the end of the run.</Callout>

And there's no prize in performance either: there isn't enough evidence that fasted training improves endurance over fed training. In long or hard sessions it usually makes it worse.

## So is it wrong?

No. If you go out first thing and running without breakfast suits you, do it: it's convenient and saves you an unsettled stomach. What you shouldn't do is force it believing it's a strategy. Run fasted because it works for you, not because you think it makes you thinner.

## What to do tomorrow

- Short, easy sessions: fasted or fed, whichever you prefer.
- Long sessions, or the day you push: eat something first. You'll run better.
- If you get dizzy, feel flat, or end up raiding the fridge when you get back, eat breakfast. That's your body handing you the data.

<Warning>If you have diabetes, take medication that lowers blood sugar, or have had dizzy spells while running, talk to your doctor before trying any of this.</Warning>

A note on method: most of these studies are short, in healthy people, and measure the acute effect. Nobody has followed a thousand runners for two years, so the conclusion is “there's no reason to believe it helps”, not “it's proven to make no difference”.
