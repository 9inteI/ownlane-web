People quit between the second and fifth week. That isn't a coincidence: it's precisely the stretch where the effort already hurts and the result isn't visible yet.

## The real timeline

- Days 1 to 10: nothing measurable changes. What changes is that starting feels easier, and that's coordination and confidence, not fitness.
- Weeks 2 to 4: this is where the good part starts, and it's invisible. Blood volume rises and the heart pumps more per beat. It shows up as “the same pace at a lower heart rate”, not as running faster.
- Weeks 6 to 12: now it shows. Studies in untrained people find aerobic capacity rising by around twenty per cent after ten to twelve weeks of running three days a week.
- After that: you keep improving, but more slowly. The big gains are the early ones.

<Callout>What you feel in week three isn't “I'm not improving”. It's “I'm improving at something that doesn't show yet”.</Callout>

## Why pace is a bad thermometer

Pace depends on heat, wind, whether you slept, the gradient and how your stomach is doing. It can get worse in a week when you improved. That's why watching only pace discourages people who are doing fine.

Better signals, all free: being able to talk at a pace where you previously couldn't, recovering faster when you stop, the same session leaving you less wrecked the next day, and your longest continuous block being longer than it was a month ago.

## What to do tomorrow

- If you're in week three and see nothing, that's normal. Don't change the plan.
- Track how long you can run without stopping, not at what pace. That's the number that rises cleanly.
- Compare yourself with a month ago, not last week.
